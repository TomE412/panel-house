Kit-Built Panel House

A single-storey house built from a bolted steel kit and 100 mm IBR / EPS cold-room panels. The frame comes to site ready to bolt together; the panels lock over the steel and brace the house. Walls finish flush inside and out, and the kit can be welded out later to make it permanent.

How it goes together

  1. Customer prepares any base: slab, piles or sleepers. Each foot node has a 200 × 200 base plate, so the kit is the same whatever it stands on.
  2. Base rails and base ties bolt into the foot nodes to make a level steel base frame.
  3. Posts, eaves ties, rafters and king posts slide onto shop-welded spigot nodes: one M16 bolt per joint. Frames sit every 3.6 m. Temporary props hold the frame until the panels go on.
  4. Wall panels drop over the base rail with their 80 mm skin flaps, the top rail drops into the top flaps, and screws lock panel and steel together. The panels are what brace the house.
  5. Roof panels go over the rafters and purlin with a 300 mm eave overhang, then ridge capping, barges and trims.
  6. To make it permanent, fillet-weld each tube end to its node. The bolts stay in.

How it works

Frame, panels and joints

An 80 × 80 × 3 SHS frame sits inside the 100 mm wall panels, with a 10 mm thermal-break strip on each face (10 + 80 + 10 = 100). Roof panels sit over the rafters so the insulation is unbroken where the sun hits.

End section, typical frame

Panel lock: rail, panel, rail

The factory leaves 80 mm of skin past the foam at the top and bottom of each wall panel. The panel drops over the base rail, the top rail drops into its top flaps, and screws through both skins lock panel and steel together.

Frame spacing: 2.4 m or 3.6 m

Wider spacing means fewer frames across the ceiling and a more open room. Each frame then carries 50% more roof, so the rafters and purlins go up a size. Everything else stays the same, including the 80 mm posts inside the 100 mm walls.

Rough hand checks, not a design: 0.5 kPa roof live load plus panel weight, 6 m span, 30° roof, factored loads. At 3.6 m the 80×80×3 rafter would be about 85% used and sag about 20 mm, and the 100×50×2 purlin would sag about 20 mm. The heavier sizes bring both back to about 50–65% used. The eaves ties stay: they stop the walls spreading. An engineer still needs to check it, including wind uplift on the purlins.

Open questions

  • Can the factory leave 80 mm skin flaps past the foam, top and bottom, and at edges that land on posts?
  • Panel span: roof panel 1.7 m to the purlin plus a 300 mm overhang. Is that within the 100 mm panel's rating?
  • Engineer's check: 80 × 80 frame with panels as bracing walls, screw spacing, eaves node, hold-down.
  • Local supply of 70 × 70 × 3 SHS for spigots, or spigots made up from plate.
  • Price and availability of 50 mm panel for internal partitions.
  • At 3.6 m spacing: engineer to confirm the 80 × 80 × 4 rafter and 125 × 50 × 2.5 purlin, and the purlin bracing for wind uplift.
  • Does the 70 × 70 × 3 spigot slide into the 80 × 80 × 4 rafter (72 mm inside)? The inside weld seam may need grinding.
